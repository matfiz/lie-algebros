Lecture 3

Solvability and Lie’s theorem

Engel’s theorem produced a common null vector. We now look for a common eigenvector. For a solvable algebra, every nonzero module has one, and every module can then be put in upper triangular form. Such a module need not be a direct sum of irreducible submodules.

Throughout, Lie algebras and modules are finite-dimensional over C\CC.

Proof. If ρ(g)\rho(\g)ρ(g) is solvable, then VVV is a module for the solvable algebra ρ(g)⊆gl(V)\rho(\g)\subseteq\gl(V)ρ(g)⊆gl(V). Corollary 3.9 gives a basis that triangularises every ρ(x)\rho(x)ρ(x).

Conversely, such a basis identifies ρ(g)\rho(\g)ρ(g) with a subalgebra of bd\borel_dbd​. For X,Y∈bdX,Y\in\borel_dX,Y∈bd​, (XY)ii=XiiYii=(YX)ii,(XY)_{ii}=X_{ii}Y_{ii}=(YX)_{ii},(XY)ii​=Xii​Yii​=(YX)ii​, so [X,Y]∈nd[X,Y]\in\mathfrak{n}_d[X,Y]∈nd​. We close with an outlook. It is not used later. Over a field of characteristic zero, the radical exists by the same argument.

Theorem 3.14 (Levi decompositionC). Let g\gg be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero. There is a subalgebra s\mathfrak{s}s of g\gg with g=s⊕rad⁡g\g=\mathfrak{s}\oplus\operatorname{rad}\gg=s⊕radg as vector spaces. Summary

The derived series defines solvability. Solvability passes to subalgebras and quotients. If an ideal and the quotient by it are solvable, then so is the whole algebra. The analogous statement for nilpotency fails. Sums of solvable ideals are solvable, so the radical exists.

Lie’s theorem gives a common eigenvector in every nonzero module of a solvable algebra. Its main step is the invariance lemma, whose trace step needs characteristic zero. A representation is triangularisable exactly when its image is solvable. A triangular module need not be a direct sum of irreducible submodules.
