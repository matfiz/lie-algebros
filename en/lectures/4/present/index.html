Lecture 4

The Killing form and semisimple structure

Taking traces in a representation gives an invariant bilinear form on a Lie algebra. For the adjoint representation this is the Killing form. We prove that it detects semisimplicity and splits a semisimple algebra into simple ideals.

Throughout, Lie algebras and modules are finite-dimensional over C\CC. The radical Rad⁡g\operatorname{Rad}\gRadg is the largest solvable ideal of g\gg, as in Lecture 3. Results are marked [P] proved, [S] sketched or [C] cited.

4.1 Invariant trace forms

Definition 4.1 (Invariant form). A bilinear form β ⁣:g×g→C\beta\colon\g\times\g\to\Cβ:g×g→C is invariant if β([x,y],z)=β(x,[y,z])for all x,y,z∈g.\beta([x,y],z)=\beta(x,[y,z])\quad\text{for all }x,y,z\in\g.β([x,y],z)=β(x,[y,z])for all x,y,z∈g. For a subspace a⊆g\alga\subseteq\ga⊆g, put a⊥={x∈g:β(x,a)=0 for all a∈a}.\alga^\perp=\{x\in\g:\beta(x,a)=0\text{ for all }a\in\alga\}.a⊥={x∈g:β(x,a)=0 for all a∈a}. The radical of β\betaβ is g⊥\g^\perpg⊥. The form is nondegenerate if its radical is zero.

All forms below are symmetric. So it does not matter on which side orthogonality is tested. Do not confuse the radical g⊥\g^\perpg⊥ of a form with the radical Rad⁡g\operatorname{Rad}\gRadg of the algebra. Example 4.12 shows that they can differ.

Definition 4.2 (Trace form and Killing form). Let ρ ⁣:g→gl(V)\rho\colon\g\to\gl(V)ρ:g→gl(V) be a representation. Its trace form is βρ(x,y)=tr⁡(ρ(x)ρ(y)).\beta_\rho(x,y)=\tr\bigl(\rho(x)\rho(y)\bigr).βρ​(x,y)=tr(ρ(x)ρ(y)). We write κ\kappaκ when the algebra is clear. The Killing form depends only on the Lie algebra.

4.3 The Killing criterion

Definition 4.8 (Semisimple and simple). A Lie algebra g\gg is semisimple if Rad⁡g=0\operatorname{Rad}\g=0Radg=0, that is, if g\gg has no nonzero solvable ideal. It is simple if it is nonabelian and its only ideals are 000 and g\gg.

The zero algebra is semisimple under this convention. A simple algebra is semisimple. Indeed, [g,g][\g,\g][g,g] is an ideal, and it is nonzero because g\gg is nonabelian. So [g,g]=g[\g,\g]=\g[g,g]=g. The derived series is constant and nonzero, so g\gg is not solvable. The ideal Rad⁡g\operatorname{Rad}\gRadg is therefore not g\gg, so it is zero.

By induction it is a direct sum of simple ideals of a⊥\alga^\perpa⊥, and these are ideals of g\gg. Together with a\algaa, they give the required decomposition of g\gg.

Corollary 4.14 (Ideals of a semisimple algebra [P]). Let g\gg be semisimple, decomposed as in Theorem 4.13.

  1. [g,g]=g[\g,\g]=\g[g,g]=g.

  2. Every ideal of g\gg is the sum of some of the gi\g_igi​.

Proof. (i) Each gi\g_igi​ is simple, so [gi,gi]=gi[\g_i,\g_i]=\g_i[gi​,gi​]=gi​. Hence [g,g]⊇∑i[gi,gi]=g[\g,\g]\supseteq\sum_i[\g_i,\g_i]=\g[g,g]⊇∑i​[gi​,gi​]=g.

(ii) Let a\algaa be an ideal. The proof of Theorem 4.13 shows that a\algaa is semisimple and that its ideals are ideals of g\gg. By Theorem 4.13 applied to a\algaa, the ideal a\algaa is a direct sum of simple ideals of a\algaa. These are simple ideals of g\gg, hence among the gi\g_igi​. 

4.6 Imports for root theory

Recall from Lecture 1 that a derivation of g\gg is a linear map D ⁣:g→gD\colon\g\to\gD:g→g with D[y,z]=[Dy,z]+[y,Dz]for all y,z∈g.D[y,z]=[Dy,z]+[y,Dz]\quad\text{for all }y,z\in\g.D[y,z]=[Dy,z]+[y,Dz]for all y,z∈g. The derivations form a Lie subalgebra Der⁡g\operatorname{Der}\gDerg of gl(g)\gl(\g)gl(g). For semisimple g\gg, the centre is zero, so ad⁡\adad is injective.

Theorem 4.15 (Inner derivations [C]). Let g\gg be semisimple. Then ad⁡ ⁣:g→Der⁡g\ad\colon\g\to\operatorname{Der}\gad:g→Derg is an isomorphism of Lie algebras. (Humphreys, §5.3, Theorem; Erdmann–Wildon, Chapter 9.)

Theorem 4.16 (Abstract Jordan decomposition [C]). Let g\gg be semisimple and x∈gx\in\gx∈g. There are unique xs,xn∈gx_s,x_n\in\gxs​,xn​∈g with x=xs+xn,[xs,xn]=0,x=x_s+x_n,\qquad [x_s,x_n]=0,x=xs​+xn​,[xs​,xn​]=0, such that ad⁡xs\ad x_sadxs​ is diagonalisable and ad⁡xn\ad x_nadxn​ is nilpotent. Indeed, ad⁡xs=p(ad⁡x)\ad x_s=p(\ad x)adxs​=p(adx) for a polynomial ppp without constant term, and (ad⁡x)(y)=0(\ad x)(y)=0(adx)(y)=0. Summary

Trace forms are invariant, so orthogonal complements of ideals are ideals. Cartan’s criterion is cited with a proof sketch. It shows that a subalgebra on which the Killing form vanishes is solvable. Hence g\gg is semisimple exactly when κg\kappa_\gκg​ is nondegenerate.

A semisimple algebra is the direct sum of its simple ideals, and these are unique. Nondegeneracy is not positivity. For example, e≠0e\ne0e=0 in sl2\sllie_2sl2​, but κ(e,e)=0\kappa(e,e)=0κ(e,e)=0. Lecture 5 uses a nondegenerate trace form to build the Casimir operator.
